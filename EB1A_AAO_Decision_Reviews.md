# EB-1A / O-1 AAO Decision Reviews

**Updated:** October 7, 2026
**Scope:** USCIS Administrative Appeals Office (AAO) non-precedent decisions, 2024–2025, for EB-1A (Form I-140) — with emphasis on denials. Decisions reviewed in full: 15 (14 dismissals, 1 favorable-authored reference). O-1A decisions use the same extraordinary-ability framework (see USCIS Policy Alert PA-2025-02, Jan. 8, 2025), so the reasoning patterns below transfer to O-1A filings.

> **LEGAL STATUS — READ FIRST:** Every decision cited here is an AAO **non-precedent** decision. Non-precedent decisions apply existing law to the facts of one case; they are binding only on the parties and **do not create or modify agency guidance**. They are persuasive only, not binding — on USCIS officers or on your case. The AAO itself says so explicitly: unpublished agency decisions "are not binding, even when they are published in private publications or widely circulated" (see In Re: 34673704, citing R.L. Inv. Ltd. Partners v. INS). Use these to understand *how USCIS reasons*, never as precedent you can cite as controlling.

## PART 1 — FAVORABLE TECHNOLOGY DECISIONS (what won)

These five are the verified favorable technology decisions. Each shows what a *winning* record looks like at both Kazarian steps.

### 1. Matter of O-B-A-, June 28, 2019 — computer-science/HCI researcher
https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2019/JUN282019_01B2203.pdf
The Director credited only judging and authorship and denied. The AAO added **leading/critical role** and **sustained acclaim** on the record as a whole, reversing. Win pattern: the petitioner's research leadership role was documented through the *institution's* reliance on his work (not just his employer's praise), and the totality — not any single paper — carried final merits.

### 2. In Re: 35160496, December 9, 2024 — software developer / CTO, 3D dental scanning
https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2024/DEC092024_02B2203.pdf
The Director granted three criteria but denied at final merits. The AAO reversed, holding that **competitors need not adopt** the petitioner's patented technology for it to count — 20%+ global market share, objective trade-press coverage, and an approximately **$2 billion acquisition** of his company demonstrated commercialization and field-level recognition. Win pattern: commercial outcomes with dollar figures and independent media beat abstract "innovation" claims.

### 3. In Re: 27032681, August 14, 2023 — AI / self-driving technology engineer and entrepreneur
https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2023/AUG142023_01B2203.pdf
The Director granted only published material. The AAO added **original contributions** and **leading/critical role** based on patents, corroborated expert endorsements, media coverage, investment, and company evidence. Win pattern: patents + money + independent press, each corroborating the other, won two contested criteria and the appeal.

### 4. In Re: 28511240, November 30, 2023 — big-data-center consultant
https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2023/NOV302023_02B2203.pdf
Three criteria were granted but the Director denied final merits. The AAO found the totality — national-standards leadership, industry recognition, and responsibility for high-value financial data centers — established acclaim. Win pattern: **standards leadership and operational responsibility at national scale** can carry final merits even without patents or papers.

### 5. In Re: 12091215, November 30, 2020 — AI founder / inventor
https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2020/NOV302020_02B2203.pdf
The Director granted only published material and leading/critical role. The AAO added **original contributions**, relying on patents, a company acquisition exceeding **$10 million**, independent coverage, awards, commercial impact, and corroborated expert letters. Win pattern: the same formula as #2 and #3 — patents + acquisition dollars + independent media + specific (not generic) expert letters.

**Common thread across all five:** no winner relied on letters alone or on internal employer impact alone. Every winning record paired expert opinion with *objective, third-party, quantified* corroboration: market share, acquisition price, revenue, patents, or independent press.

## PART 2 — DENIAL PATTERNS (why petitions fail)

Distilled from 14 dismissed appeals reviewed in full (2024–2025). Each pattern is anchored to a real decision — name, date, and verified USCIS URL.

### Pattern 1 — Conclusory recommendation letters with no objective corroboration
*In Re: 34673704 (AI researcher), Dec. 23, 2024 — https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2024/DEC232024_01B2203.pdf*

The petitioner submitted **seven support letters**. The AAO found they "generally offer praise for the Petitioner's skills and accomplishments" but "do not specify the significance of the Petitioner's contributions to his field." Two more problems: the authors were his former professors and his current employer — **not independent** — and USCIS "need not accept primarily conclusory statements" (citing 1756, Inc. v. U.S. Att'y Gen.). For "major significance," the AAO requires evidence the work was **"widely adopted or replicated by people in the field who are unaffiliated with the petitioner"** (citing Amin v. Mayorkas, 5th Cir.).

The companion lesson from *In Re: 35367285 (software developer/entrepreneur), Feb. 3, 2025* (https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2025/FEB032025_01B2203.pdf): "multiple letters from industry leaders" were found to establish only "business interest" in his software platform — not acclaim. And bare assertions in a brief are **not evidence** (citing Matter of S-M-, 22 I&N Dec. 49).

**Rule:** A letter that says you are skilled, talented, or a "true genius" — without specifying *what* you contributed, *who* outside your circle adopted it, and *how* the field changed — counts for nothing. Letters must come from independent experts and be corroborated by objective evidence.

### Pattern 2 — Meeting three criteria but failing final merits ("useful work ≠ sustained acclaim")
*In Re: 35367285 (software developer/entrepreneur), Feb. 3, 2025 — URL above*

This is the single most dangerous pattern. The Director **granted three criteria** (judging, authorship, leading/critical role). The AAO still dismissed at Kazarian step 2: the petitioner had written two SAP textbooks (5,900 copies sold — but **no comparative data** ranking sales within the field), built an ERP platform, and had a proposed $1M acquisition — but the agreement was **unsigned**, the acquisition never shown to have closed, and the CEO offer letter came from a company where **his spouse was president**. The AAO's holding: the record did not "differentiate the petitioner amongst other similarly advancing SAP computer platform developers/leaders." Customer letters proved the software was *useful*; usefulness is not acclaim.

The AI researcher (In Re: 34673704) failed at step 1 (only 2 of 10 criteria), but the AAO added the same aggregate warning: the classification is for those "already at the top of their respective fields, rather than for individuals progressing toward the top."

**Rule:** Three checked boxes do not win. Final merits asks whether the *totality* shows a "career of acclaimed work" placing you in the small percentage at the very top. Employer-internal impact, no matter how real, must be translated into *field-level* recognition.

### Pattern 3 — Citations and authorship without field-relative context
*In Re: 34673704 (AI researcher), Dec. 23, 2024 — URL above; In Re: 35392583, Dec. 9, 2024 — https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2024/DEC092024_01B2203.pdf; Matter of C (pharmacist), Dec. 16, 2024 — https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2024/DEC162024_01B2203.pdf*

Three sub-patterns:
- **Raw citation counts are not enough.** The AI researcher's citations were acknowledged, but he "has not submitted evidence to demonstrate that a good number of researchers have been sufficiently influenced by his work such that it rises to the level of major significance." Citations must be shown to be *high relative to others in the field*, with evidence that citing works relied heavily on the petitioner's findings.
- **No field-relative benchmark = no finding.** In In Re: 35392583, the petitioner ranked himself 4th of six scholars — but the comparison list was "only one researcher's opinion," and he had submitted the RFE-requested comparison evidence **late, on appeal** (disregarded under Matter of Soriano).
- **The venue must be proven "major."** In Matter of C, the petitioner's articles were real and verifiable — but she "has not provided evidence that the journals constitute major trade publications." A major trade publication must be shown to be "one of the major publications in the field" (citing Braga v. Poulos).

**Rule:** For authorship, prove three things: (1) the work exists, (2) the venue is selective/major (acceptance rates, impact factor, field standing), (3) the citations are high *relative to field and year norms* — with the comparison done by an independent source, submitted on time.

### Pattern 4 — Memberships that don't require outstanding achievement
*In Re: 34673704 (AI researcher), Dec. 23, 2024; In Re: 34922903 (lawyer/legal entrepreneur), Feb. 5, 2025 — https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2025/FEB052025_01B2203.pdf*

The AI researcher claimed AAAI, IAENG, IEEE Computer Society, and ISAC. The AAO examined the bylaws: AAAI membership requires only "acceptance of an application" plus dues; his IEEE Computer Society certificate said merely "affiliate in good standing"; ISAC membership "is open to all persons." One claimed membership was still "being processed" at filing. The lawyer's bar-association memberships failed identically — no evidence of outstanding-achievement requirements judged by experts.

**Rule:** Submit the association's bylaws or official criteria and highlight the exact language requiring outstanding achievement *as judged by recognized experts*. Plain IEEE/AAAI/ACM membership does not qualify; selective grades (e.g., IEEE Senior Member, ACM Distinguished) with documented election criteria do. The membership must exist *at filing*.

### Pattern 5 — Leading/critical role without organizational proof
*In Re: 34673704 (AI researcher), Dec. 23, 2024; In Re: 33403917 (research scientist), Aug. 29, 2024 — https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/err/B2%20-%20Aliens%20with%20Extraordinary%20Ability/Decisions_Issued_in_2024/AUG292024_01B2203.pdf*

The AI researcher's "Director of ICT" title came with a duties list — but **no organizational chart** showing where he sat in the hierarchy, and no evidence his work was "of significant importance to the outcome of the organization's activities." His employer's letter calling him "a true genius in the field of AI" was dismissed as conclusory. Separately, his employer — despite holding government contracts — was not shown to have a "distinguished reputation" (no media coverage, no customer-base evidence). The research scientist's postdoctoral fellow role failed the same way.

**Rule:** For leading/critical role, submit (1) an org chart or equivalent showing your position, (2) a detailed letter explaining *how* your work drove organizational outcomes (not adjectives), and (3) independent evidence of the organization's distinguished reputation (media, scale, rankings).

### Pattern 6 — High salary without comparative surveys
*In Re: 33403917 (research scientist), Aug. 29, 2024 — URL above*

W-2s, offer letters, and employer recommendation letters were **insufficient by themselves**. "Above average" is not "high": the AAO requires compensation high *relative to others in the field*, proven with "geographical or position-appropriate compensation surveys." Her evidence failed each test: New Scientist/NIH figures covered only NIH postdocs; Salary.com data was user-reported (unreliable) and her pay sat near its average anyway; entry-level LCA prevailing-wage figures said nothing about what is "high" for similarly experienced workers.

**Rule:** The high-salary criterion is won with apples-to-apples comparisons: DOL/BLS or reputable survey data matched to occupation, experience level, and geography — showing the petitioner at or near the top of the distribution, not merely above its middle.

### Pattern 7 — Timing and procedural traps
Across the reviewed decisions:
- **Eligibility is judged at filing.** Post-filing citation growth cannot retroactively establish eligibility (In Re: 34673704, citing 8 C.F.R. § 103.2(b)(1) and Matter of Katigbak).
- **New evidence on appeal is disregarded** if the petitioner had notice and a reasonable opportunity to submit it earlier — e.g., after an RFE specifically requested it (In Re: 35392583, citing Matter of Soriano).
- **Issues not raised on appeal are waived** (In Re: 33403917, citing Matter of O-R-E-).
- **Awards must be national/international in scale.** The lawyer in In Re: 34922903 *conceded* her recognitions were "not on a national or international scale" — criterion failed on her own admission.

## PART 3 — PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS FOR A SOFTWARE/AI PETITIONER'S FILE

1. **Write every letter to the Amin standard.** Each expert letter must name the specific contribution, state who unaffiliated with you adopted/replicated/cited it, and explain the field-level effect. Ban adjectives ("genius," "pioneer") unless followed by facts. Use independent experts — not your managers, professors, or reports.
2. **Benchmark every number.** Citations → field- and year-normalized percentile (not raw counts). Salary → DOL/BLS or reputable survey, matched on occupation + experience + geography, showing top-of-distribution. Book/product sales → rank within the field, not just units. Downloads/users → comparison set.
3. **Prove venue selectivity.** For each paper or article: acceptance rate, impact factor or equivalent, and the venue's standing in the field. A paper in an unproven venue is a weak criterion-6 claim (Matter of C).
4. **Document memberships with bylaws.** Highlight the exact outstanding-achievement + expert-judgment language. Pursue selective grades (IEEE Senior Member and above), not open memberships.
5. **Build the leading-role packet as a unit:** org chart + detailed outcome-focused letter + independent proof of the employer's distinguished reputation (press, rankings, customer scale).
6. **Translate employer impact into field impact.** The software-developer denial (In Re: 35367285) is the cautionary tale: real products, real customers, real revenue — dismissed, because nothing showed *the field* recognized him as distinct from his peers. Commercialization evidence (market share, acquisition price, adoption by unaffiliated parties) is what crosses that line — it is exactly what won In Re: 35160496 and In Re: 12091215.
7. **File only when the record is complete.** Citations earned after filing don't count; evidence held back past an RFE may be disregarded on appeal. The initial filing must be independently approvable (see also the Aug. 5, 2026 USCIS policy permitting denial without RFE/NOID).
8. **Never submit unsigned, incomplete, or self-dealing documents.** The unsigned acquisition agreement and the spouse-owned-company offer letter in In Re: 35367285 actively damaged credibility. One bad exhibit can taint the whole record.
9. **At final merits, tell a time-deep story.** The statute says *sustained* acclaim. Spread recognition across years, diversify sources (not one employer's ecosystem), and show the trajectory is continuing — not a single peak.
